How to Decode Morse Code From Dots and Dashes

Learn to turn short signals, long signals, and the spaces between them into real words. From your first .- to listening like a radio operator.

A Morse message can look like a maze of periods and hyphens: .... . .-.. .-.. ---. But once you know what each group represents—and why pauses matter—those marks turn into a readable word: HELLO.

Quick answer

To decode Morse code, first separate the signals into letters using spaces or pauses. Read each group of dots (.) and dashes (-) with an International Morse code chart: .... is H, . is E, and .-.. is L. A longer pause—or a slash in typed Morse—indicates a new word.

01 The foundations

What do dots and dashes actually represent?

Morse code encodes characters as sequences of two signal lengths: a short mark called a dit (dot), and a longer mark called a dah (dash). In printed text, we usually write them as . and -. Over a radio, telegraph wire, buzzer, or light, they are periods of signal followed by meaningful silence. A code group such as -.-. is the letter C.

This guide uses International Morse code, the familiar alphabet standardized in International Telecommunication Union Recommendation ITU-R M.1677-1. That is different from the older American Morse code associated with nineteenth-century landline telegraphy. The modern international system is the right reference for most alphabet charts, amateur radio, and online converters.

The code evolved with practical electric telegraphy. Samuel F. B. Morse and Alfred Vail played central roles in its early development; Leonard Gale also helped develop the telegraph system. The Library of Congress and Smithsonian Institution document their work. What matters for decoding today is the remarkably simple principle that a small set of signals can represent an entire message.

02 From symbols to words

How to decode Morse code in six simple steps

Start with a neatly separated, written message. You do not need to memorize every letter first; a reference chart is enough to read your first sentence. Then gradually train yourself to recognize whole patterns without looking them up.

Identify the marks. Read a dot as a short signal and a dash as a long signal. Some sources use a middle dot · and a long bar −; they represent the same idea.

Locate character boundaries. In neatly typed Morse, one blank space usually separates letter groups. In a real signal, a three-unit pause separates one letter from the next.

Locate word boundaries. A slash (/) commonly separates words in written Morse. A transmitted signal normally uses a seven-unit pause instead of a slash symbol.

Look up each group. Use the A–Z, 0–9, and punctuation chart below. For example, --. means G; .--. means P; and ....- means 4.

Combine letters and restore words. Translate the groups in their original order. Preserve punctuation, and use the longer gaps to reconstruct the message.

Check doubtful groups. If a group is unknown, recheck the dot–dash sequence and pause length. One missing dash can change a letter completely; do not force a guess.

Worked example: decode HELLO WORLD

Suppose someone sends .... . .-.. .-.. --- / .-- --- .-. .-.. -... Read each separated group in sequence. The first five groups become H E L L O; the slash marks the start of a new word; the final five groups become W O R L D.

03 Timing and pauses

Why the silence between dots and dashes matters

Correct decoding depends on duration and spacing, not just which marks appear. Each dot lasts one basic unit. A dash lasts three units. The gap between elements within one letter is one unit, the gap between letters is three units, and the gap between words is seven units. These relative timings stay the same whether you transmit quickly or slowly.

For instance, the letter A is .-: a short signal, one unit of silence, then a long signal. After A finishes, a longer three-unit letter gap tells you that the next sequence starts a different character. Without this distinction, a stream of dots and dashes can have multiple interpretations.

Signal or gapLengthWhat it tells the reader
Dot / dit1 unitShort mark
Dash / dah3 unitsLong mark
Between elements1 unitContinue the same character
Between letters3 unitsStart a new character
Between words7 unitsStart a new word

Radio operators measure sending speed in words per minute (WPM). The conventional reference word PARIS, including the following word gap, takes 50 timing units, so the duration of one dot in milliseconds is approximately 1200 ÷ WPM. At 12 WPM, a dot is 100 milliseconds and a dash is 300 milliseconds. Farnsworth spacing slows the gaps between characters or words while keeping the characters themselves at a higher speed, giving beginners more time to recognize each sound.

Begin with the common short groups: E (.), T (-), A (.-), N (-.), I (..), and M (--). The five-element number patterns are also intuitive: 1 begins with one dot, 5 is five dots, 6 begins with one dash, and 0 is five dashes.

The period (.-.-.-), comma (--..--), question mark (..--..), slash (-..-.), and at sign (.--.-.) are useful examples. Note that an actual slash punctuation character has its own transmitted code; the slash you type between Morse words is only a convenient notation for a long pause.

05 Decode without scanning a chart

Use a Morse decoding tree to find letters faster

An alternative to memorizing a whole table is a binary-style Morse decoding tree. Start at the top, move left for every dot and right for every dash, and stop after the last mark. For example, -.- leads right to T, left to N, then right to K. The final letter is K.

The tree illustrates why letter lengths vary: E and T use only one mark, while Q, Y, V, and many other letters use four. Do not confuse this branching diagram with a system that can recover missing boundaries automatically: the tree still needs to know where each character ends.

Try .-- by following left, right, right. You arrive at W. Try --.. with right, right, left, left. You arrive at Z. After several repetitions, your brain starts to recognize each complete pattern as a single sound or shape.

07 Recognize a famous signal

How to decode SOS and other familiar Morse patterns

One of the best-known Morse patterns is SOS: three short marks, three long marks, and three short marks. The letters S and O are easy to identify because S is ... and O is ---. In a classroom transcription, you may see the sequence as ... --- ....

Technically, the standardized SOS distress signal is a continuous procedural sign sent without the normal three-unit letter gaps inside it. That is why the uninterrupted notation ...---... matters too. In this page's decoder, that exact continuous sequence is recognized as the special SOS case, but arbitrary unspaced signals are not automatically broken into letters.
